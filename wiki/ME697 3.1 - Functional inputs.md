# ME697 3.1 - Functional inputs

**Summary**: Scientific models often take whole functions as inputs (coefficients, forcing, boundary data) and return functions. Uncertainty in such an input is a random field, usually a Gaussian process. Surrogates on the raw discretized field hit the curse of dimensionality, so the lecture reduces dimension: SVD and [[Principal component analysis]] for data, the [[Karhunen-Loève expansion]] for Gaussian random fields. Two worked examples propagate a log-Gaussian conductivity field through the 1D steady heat equation, first to one number, then to the whole temperature field.

## Key results
> [!definition] Solver as operator (01)
> For $-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla u)=f$ in $\Omega$ with $u=g$ on $\partial\Omega$, the solver is a map
> $$S:\mathcal A\times\mathcal F\times\mathcal G\to\mathcal U,\qquad (a,f,g)\mapsto u$$
> between function spaces. Maps with functional inputs and outputs are **operators** (see [[Operator learning]]).

- **Random-field inputs (01)**: model a positive conductivity as $a=\exp\{h\}$ with $h\sim\mathrm{GP}(m,k)$. The mean function encodes trends, the covariance encodes smoothness ([[Gaussian process]]).
- **Monte Carlo (01)**: sample $\mathbf h\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf m,\mathbf C)$ at finite-element nodes, solve, repeat.
- **Curse of dimensionality (01)**: [[Polynomial chaos]], neural networks and GPs trained directly on the discretized field need very many samples. Remedies: reduce input dimension (this lecture) or learn the operator ([[ME697 3.2 - Operator learning]]).

> [!definition] Singular value decomposition (02)
> For $\mathbf X\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}$:
> $$\mathbf X=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf V^{T}$$
> Truncated at rank $k$:
> $$\mathbf X_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^{T}$$
> This is the best rank-$k$ approximation in the Frobenius norm. Choose $k$ so that the retained fraction of $\sum\sigma_i^2$ reaches a target (0.998 for the image demo). Storing the rank-$k$ factors takes $(n+m)k$ numbers instead of $nm$. See [[Singular value decomposition]].

> [!theorem] PCA from SVD (03)
> Center the data, $\mathbf B=\mathbf X-\bar{\mathbf x}$, with covariance
> $$\mathbf C=\tfrac1n\mathbf B^{T}\mathbf B$$
> The first principal direction maximizes $\mathbf v^{T}\mathbf C\mathbf v$ over unit vectors, which gives the top eigenvector of $\mathbf C$ (Lagrange multipliers). With $\mathbf B=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf V^{T}$:
> $$\mathbf C=\tfrac1n\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Sigma^2\mathbf V^{T}$$
> So columns of $\mathbf V$ are the principal directions and $\sigma_j^2/n$ is the variance along direction $j$. Total variance is $\sum_j\sigma_j^2/n$. The principal components (scores) are
> $$\mathbf Z=\mathbf B\mathbf V=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma$$
> and they are uncorrelated: $\langle z_{ik}z_{il}\rangle=\tfrac{\sigma_k^2}{n}\delta_{kl}$. MNIST demo: directions are digit-like images, and the first two components separate digit classes. See [[Principal component analysis]].

> [!definition] Karhunen-Loève expansion of a GP (04)
> For $f\sim\mathrm{GP}(m,k)$:
> $$f(\mathbf x)=m(\mathbf x)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\xi_i\sqrt{\lambda_i}\,\phi_i(\mathbf x)$$
> with independent $\xi_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $(\lambda_i,\phi_i)$ the eigenpairs of the covariance operator,
> $$\int k(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')\phi_i(\mathbf x')\,d\mathbf x'=\lambda_i\phi_i(\mathbf x)$$
> Pointwise variance is $\sum_i\lambda_i\phi_i^2(\mathbf x)$ and the field "energy" is
> $$\mathcal E[f]=\int\mathbb V[f(\mathbf x)]\,d\mathbf x=\sum_i\lambda_i$$
> Truncate at the smallest $M$ with $\sum_{i\le M}\lambda_i\ge\alpha\sum_i\lambda_i$, typically $\alpha=0.95$. An infinite-dimensional field becomes $M$ standard normals.

> [!algorithm] Nystrom KLE (04)
> 1. Quadrature points $\mathbf x_q$ with weights $w_q$ (uniform grid on $[0,1]$ or $[0,1]^2$).
> 2. Eigendecompose the discretized operator $K_{qq'}w_{q'}$.
> 3. Keep eigenvalues until the cumulative energy exceeds $\alpha$.
> 4. Extend eigenfunctions off the grid by $\phi_i(\mathbf x)=\lambda_i^{-1}\sum_q v_{qi}\sqrt{w_q}\,k(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_q)$.
> For a GP conditioned on data, the same code expands the posterior GP.

- **Lengthscale vs decay (04)**: larger lengthscale (smoother samples) means faster eigenvalue decay, because the leading eigenfunctions are the smoothest ones.
- **Rough kernels need many terms (04)**: with the exponential kernel and $\alpha=0.8$ the sampled fields look too smooth. The truncation, not the kernel, removed the roughness.
- **With observations (04)**: the posterior eigenfunctions are pinned near zero at observed inputs.

> [!example] Stochastic heat equation to one number (05)
> Steady rod $\frac{d}{dx}\big(c\,\frac{du}{dx}\big)=0$, $u(0)=1$, $u(1)=0$. Conductivity $c=\exp\{g\}$, $g\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,\,0.5\,k_{SE})$ with lengthscale 0.1, KLE with $\alpha=0.95$ on 1000 quadrature points. Solver: finite volume (FiPy, 500 cells). Quantity of interest: $u(0.5)$.
> 1. Draw 200 samples of $\boldsymbol\xi$, solve, keep $u(0.5)$; 80/20 train/test split.
> 2. Fit an ARD squared-exponential GP from $\boldsymbol\xi$ to $u(0.5)$ by minibatch marginal likelihood with [[Adam]] (1000 steps, lr 0.01, batch 100).
> 3. The parity plot is good. Push 2000 new $\boldsymbol\xi$ through the cheap GP for a histogram of $u(0.5)$.

> [!example] Stochastic heat equation to a whole field (06)
> Same input model. Collect 100 temperature fields, hold out 10%, and run PCA (energy 0.95) on the 90 training fields. Train one GP per PCA coefficient $\eta_j$ on $\boldsymbol\xi\mapsto\eta_j$. Reconstruct
> $$u(x)\approx\bar u(x)+\sum_{j}\eta_j\phi^{\text{PCA}}_j(x)$$
> For 1000 samples of $\boldsymbol\xi$ this gives the mean field and a 95% band for $u(x)$, in a fraction of a second. This propagates uncertainty from one infinite-dimensional random field to another.

## Wiki notes (possible errors in the book)
- **Eigen-equation typos (04)**: the KLE equation is printed with $\phi_i(\mathbf x')$ on the right (should be $\phi_i(\mathbf x)$), and the orthonormality integral mixes $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x'$. In 01 the covariance matrix is called $\mathbf K$ in the sampling law and $\mathbf C$ in the definition.
- **Unsquared singular values (03 and 06)**: the MNIST and `PrincipalComponentAnalysis` code take the cumulative sum of $\sigma_j$, not $\sigma_j^2$, for "explained variance". The text of 02 and 03 says variance is $\sigma_j^2/n$. With unsquared values, "90%" and "95%" retain less variance than claimed, so too many components are kept. Notebook 04 correctly uses eigenvalues $\lambda_i$.
- **Sample-size claim (03)**: the text says "typically $n\ll m$"; MNIST has $n=70000$ samples and $m=784$ features.
- **Scores (03)**: $\mathbf Z=\mathbf X\mathbf V=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma$ holds for the centered $\mathbf B$, not for uncentered $\mathbf X$.
- **Off-by-one (02, 03, 06)**: `best_k = argmax(cum_var > alpha)` is a zero-based index, so $k$ = `best_k + 1` components are needed; the plotted label reports `best_k`. Also the "first 10 principal directions" cell plots 25.
- **Steady, not transient (05)**: step 3 says solve "for time $t=0.5$"; the problem is steady and the point is $x=0.5$.
- **Missing $c_0$ (05, 06)**: the text defines $c=c_0\exp\{g\}$ but the code sets $c=\exp\{\text{KLE}\}$ (so $c_0=1$). The displayed expansion $c(x)\approx\sum\xi_i\sqrt{\lambda_i}\phi_i$ in 06 is really for $g=\log c$, and the output expansion omits the PCA mean.
- **Minibatch with replacement (05, 06)**: `jr.randint` draws indices with replacement, and batch size 100 exceeds the 90 training points in 06 (and nearly the 160 in 05). Duplicate inputs plus a $10^{-6}$ jitter make the marginal likelihood poorly conditioned. In 06 the same `subkey` is reused for every coefficient's GP.
- **Nystrom asymmetry (04)**: the matrix $K_{qq'}w_{q'}$ is symmetric only for uniform weights, which is what the code uses; for general weights, symmetrize with $w^{1/2}Kw^{1/2}$ before `eigh`. The default grid `linspace(0,1,nq)` with weights $1/nq$ is a crude rectangle rule.
- **Ellipticity (01)**: a log-Gaussian coefficient is positive but not bounded away from zero, so the elliptic theory needs extra conditions **[unverified]**.
- **Exact answer available (05)**: in 1D, $c\,u'$ is constant, so $u(x)=1-\int_0^x c^{-1}\big/\int_0^1 c^{-1}$ (Wiki note: derived here, handy to check the FiPy solver).
