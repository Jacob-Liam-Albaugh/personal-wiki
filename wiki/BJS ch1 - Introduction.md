# BJS ch1 - Introduction

**Summary**: Defines the linear programming problem, its four modeling assumptions, how to convert between equivalent forms (standard, canonical), models and examples, the geometric solution in two variables, and the requirement space view in which feasibility means that $\mathbf b$ lies in the cone generated by the columns of $\mathbf A$. See [[Linear programming]].

**Authors**: Mokhtar S. Bazaraa, John J. Jarvis, Hanif D. Sherali · **Year**: 2010 · **File**: `Linear Optimization/BJS full book.pdf` (printed pp. 1-43). Cites use printed pages; pdf = printed + 16.

## The linear programming problem (§1.1, p. 1-6)
History (p. 1): the problem was conceived by Dantzig around 1947 while advising the US Air Force on planning; Kantorovich had formulated and solved such a problem in 1939, but his work was unknown until 1959; the term "linear programming" was coined by Koopmans in 1948; the simplex method was published in 1949.

A linear program minimizes (or maximizes) a linear objective subject to linear constraints (p. 2, image seen):
$$\min\ c_1x_1+\dots+c_nx_n$$
$$\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}x_j\ge b_i,\ \ i=1,\dots,m$$
$$x_1,\dots,x_n\ge0$$
The $x_j$ are decision variables, the $a_{ij}$ technological coefficients forming the constraint matrix $\mathbf A$, $\mathbf b$ the right-hand-side vector, $x_j\ge0$ the nonnegativity constraints. A point satisfying all constraints is a feasible solution; the set of all such points is the feasible region. Example 1.1 (p. 2): minimize $2x_1+5x_2$ s.t. $x_1+x_2\ge6$, $-x_1-2x_2\ge-18$, $x_1,x_2\ge0$.

**Assumptions** (p. 3-4): proportionality (contribution to cost and constraints is linear in the activity level, no economies of scale or setup costs), additivity (no interaction between activities), divisibility (fractional levels allowed), deterministic coefficients. Dantzig's reply to Hotelling's objection that the world is nonlinear (via von Neumann) is that LP is a model on postulated axioms and applies exactly when the situation fits them (p. 4).

**Problem manipulation** (p. 4-5, image seen at p. 5):
- Inequalities become equalities with slack or surplus variables.
- A free variable is split as $x_j=x_j'-x''$ with $x_j',x''\ge0$; for $k$ free variables one shared $x''$ suffices. Alternatively eliminate it by substitution, though that is seldom used numerically.
- A lower bound $x_j\ge\ell_j$ is handled by $x_j'=x_j-\ell_j$; an upper bound $x_j\le u_j$ by $x_j'=u_j-x_j$.
- Maximize and minimize are interchanged by
$$\max\sum c_jx_j=-\min\sum(-c_j)x_j$$

**Standard and canonical forms** (p. 5). *Standard format*: all restrictions are equalities and all variables nonnegative. The simplex method is designed for it ([[Simplex method]]). *Canonical form*: minimization with all constraints $\ge$ and nonnegative variables (or maximization with all $\le$), useful for duality ([[LP duality]]). In matrix notation the standard form is min $\mathbf c'\mathbf x$ s.t. $\mathbf A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$, $\mathbf x\ge\mathbf0$.

## Geometric solution (§1.3, p. 18-22)
For two variables, draw the feasible region as an intersection of half-planes and move a line of constant objective value $z$ in the improving direction until it last touches the region. Four outcomes (p. 20-22, Figures 1.1-1.8):
- a unique optimal solution at an extreme point,
- alternative optima (a whole edge, bounded or unbounded region),
- unbounded optimal objective value,
- an empty feasible region (inconsistent constraints, Figure 1.8).

An optimum, if it exists, is attained at an extreme point of the region, which motivates the simplex method ([[BJS ch3 - The simplex method]], [[Polyhedron]]).

## The requirement space (§1.4, p. 22-27)
For the standard form with columns $\mathbf a_1,\dots,\mathbf a_n$ (p. 23, read from text and Figure 1.9):
$$\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf a_jx_j=\mathbf b,\quad x_j\ge0$$
The set $\{\sum\mathbf a_jx_j:x_j\ge0\}$ is the cone generated by the columns. The problem is feasible iff $\mathbf b$ lies in this cone, hence the name "requirement space" ($\mathbf b$ reflects requirements to be satisfied). Among all representations, choose the one minimizing $\sum c_jx_j$. Example 1.5 (p. 27): minimizing $-2x_1-3x_2$ subject to $x_1+2x_2\ge2$ and nonnegativity is unbounded, seen in the requirement space because points in the cone with arbitrarily small $z$ exist (Figure 1.13).
