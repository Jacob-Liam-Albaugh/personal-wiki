# ME697 2.4 - Multi-fidelity modeling

**Summary**: [[Multi-fidelity modeling]] builds a surrogate of an expensive high-fidelity model $f_h$ from a lot of cheap low-fidelity data $f_l$ and a few high-fidelity evaluations. Two Gaussian-process constructions: the Kennedy-O'Hagan scale-plus-discrepancy model, and a nonlinear one that feeds the low-fidelity posterior mean into the kernel. Illustrated on a synthetic 2D function and on flow past a cylinder.

**File**: `advanced-scientific-machine-learning/book/up/mf/`

---

## Topics
- **Intro**: use a cheap approximation (e.g. a coarse-mesh finite element model) to learn a surrogate of the expensive one.
- **01 Basics**: sources of fidelity (fine vs coarse mesh, FE model vs analytic model, experiment vs analytic model); notation; the target posterior; two GP constructions; parameter fitting.
- **02 MF GPs (hands-on)**: low-fidelity GP, multi-fidelity GP via a `tinygp` transform, comparison with a high-fidelity-only GP.

## Key results
> [!definition] Target (01)
> Everything we can say about $f_h$ given all data, by marginalizing the low-fidelity function:
> $$p(f_h\mid \mathbf y_l,\mathbf y_h)=\int p(f_h\mid f_l,\mathbf y_l,\mathbf y_h)\,p(f_l\mid\mathbf y_l)\,df_l$$
> Needs (1) regression for $f_l$ (a [[Gaussian process]] with noisy data) and (2) a model linking $f_h$ to $f_l$. Inputs $\mathbf X_l,\mathbf X_h$ are left implicit.

> [!algorithm] Kennedy-O'Hagan model (01)
> $$f_h(x)=\rho f_l(x)+\epsilon(x),\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathrm{GP}(m_\epsilon,k_\epsilon)$$
> with $\epsilon$ independent of $f_l$ and $\rho$ a scalar (or a basis expansion $\rho(x)=\sum_iw_i\phi_i(x)$). After seeing the low-fidelity data, $f_h$ is again a GP:
> $$m_{h|y_l}(x)=\rho\,\tilde m_l(x)$$
> $$c_{h|y_l}(x,x')=\rho^2\tilde k_l(x,x')+k_\epsilon(x,x')$$
> For input-dependent $\rho$ replace $\rho^2$ by $\rho(x)\rho(x')$. Condition on $\mathbf y_h$ with the usual GP formulas, using $c_{h|y_l}$ in place of the prior kernel.
> (source: `01_mf_basics.md`)

- **Fitting**: maximize $\log p(\mathbf y_l,\mathbf y_h)=\log p(\mathbf y_l)+\log p(\mathbf y_h\mid\mathbf y_l)$, both terms analytic. With much more low-fidelity data, fit the low-fidelity parameters on the first term alone, then the rest on the second.
- **Nonlinear alternative** (Karumuri et al. 2023): $f_h\mid f_l$ is a GP whose kernel acts on the input and the low-fidelity output,
$$k_h(x,x')=k\big((x,f_l(x)),(x',f_l(x'))\big).$$
  This is a one-hidden-layer deep GP (Damianou et al. 2013 for general inference, which is hard). With plenty of low-fidelity data, the posterior of $f_l$ collapses to its mean, so use
$$k_h(x,x')=k\big((x,\tilde m_l(x)),(x',\tilde m_l(x'))\big).$$
  In `tinygp` this is a `Transform` that appends $\tilde m_l(x)$ to the input, with its own length scale.

## Examples
> [!example] Synthetic 2D function (02)
> $f_h$ is a sum of a squared sine and an exponentially damped squared cosine; $f_\ell$ is a scaled $f_h$ plus extra terms (modified from Perdikaris et al. 2015). Space-filling data from [[Latin hypercube sampling]]. The low-fidelity GP fits $f_\ell$ but misses $f_h$; the multi-fidelity GP is clearly the most accurate, better than a GP on the high-fidelity data alone. Suggested exercises: vary the numbers of low/high data, find the $N_h$ where the high-only GP catches up, and how different $f_\ell$ may be.

> [!example] Flow past a cylinder (02)
> Random inflow profile with two Gaussian variables; quantity of interest is the superquantile risk $\mathcal R_{0.6}[C_{BP}]$ of the base pressure coefficient (mean of the upper 40%). 8 high-fidelity and 99 low-fidelity runs. The multi-fidelity GP again has the best parity plot and its response surface almost coincides with the truth.

## Wiki notes (possible errors in the book)
- **Definition mismatch (02)**: the markdown gives $f_\ell=1.5f_h+\tfrac13\sin(x_1+x_2)+\tfrac12e^{-x_1}\sin(x_1+7x_2)$, but the code uses $2.5f_h+\tfrac13\big(\sin(x_1+x_2)+\tfrac12e^{-x_1}\sin(x_1+7x_2)\big)$. The scale (1.5 vs 2.5) and the factor on the last term differ.
- **Conditioning label (01)**: "$f_h\mid f_l=\mathrm{GP}(\tilde m_h,\tilde k_h)$" with mean $\rho\tilde m_l$ is already the result after integrating out $f_l$ given $\mathbf y_l$. Conditional on $f_l$ itself the mean is $\rho f_l+m_\epsilon$ and the covariance is $k_\epsilon$.
- The Kennedy-O'Hagan reference is cited by the book as 2000 (Biometrika 87).
