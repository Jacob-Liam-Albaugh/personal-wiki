# LP in practice

**Intuition**: large LPs are written compactly in a modeling language, solved by a tuned simplex or interior point code, and made tractable by exploiting structure: preprocessing, good pivoting rules, a strong formulation, and cutting planes when constraints are exponentially many (BT ch. 12).

## Modeling languages and libraries

Large problems have thousands to millions of variables, so constraints must be generated by a program from a compact algebraic description (BT p. 534). Modeling languages (GAMS, AMPL) turn such a description into $\mathbf A,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ and call a solver, cutting the time from model to solution (p. 535, Example 12.1: the 100 by 100 assignment constraints in a few GAMS lines).

Solver performance (Table 12.1, p. 536) shows LPs with up to millions of variables solved in minutes to hours on 1990s machines. Performance depends on the number of constraints, the sparsity of $\mathbf A$, the numerical linear algebra, and the data structures. Observations on simplex against interior point (p. 537):
- Simplex returns basic solutions. Interior point methods return a point in the interior of the optimal set, so there is no basis for reoptimization or [[LP sensitivity analysis]]. A common route for large sparse problems is interior point first, then a conversion to a basic solution (the crossover problem) and simplex for reoptimization.
- Interior point methods are sensitive to an unbounded optimal set, simplex to degeneracy.
- Interior point methods can need far more memory, depending on the sparsity of the Cholesky factors ([[Interior point method]]).

## Fleet assignment

Given a flight schedule and fleets $f\in F$ with $S(f)$ aircraft each, assign a fleet type to every flight (BT §12.3, p. 537-544). Binary $x_{fodt}$ says fleet $f$ flies the flight $(o,d,t)$, and integer $y_{fot}$ counts aircraft on the ground at a city. The constraints (p. 538-539, read from text, the displayed system was garbled in the scan) are:
- every flight is flown by exactly one fleet,
- conservation of aircraft at each city and time (a flow conservation as in [[Network flow problem]]),
- the number of aircraft of each type in use is at most $S(f)$,
- integrality.

The case study reports what mattered in practice (p. 539-544, Tables 12.3-12.8):
- **Preprocessing** (algebraic elimination of fixed variables, empty rows, redundant rows) cuts the model size and time substantially.
- **Pivoting rules**: dual steepest edge simplex beat primal Devex and primal steepest edge on these instances (Table 12.4).
- **Interior point** needs far fewer iterations, but a crossover is needed to extract a basic solution, since optimal basic solutions often have many 0 or 1 entries and help branching (p. 541-542).
- **Branching rules** specific to the problem cut the branch and bound tree from thousands of nodes to under a hundred (Table 12.6).
- Overall the combined strategy of interior point LP, crossover, dual simplex and tailored branch and bound solved the instances in under an hour (Table 12.8, p. 544).

## Air traffic flow management

Congestion creates delays; the model assigns ground and airborne delays to flights to respect sector capacities (BT §12.4, p. 544-551). The key modeling choice (p. 547) is a variable defined "by time $t$" rather than "at time $t$":
$$w_{ft}^j=1\ \ \text{if flight }f\text{ arrives at sector }j\text{ by time }t$$
The point-event variable is recovered by a difference (p. 548):
$$u_{ft}^j=w_{ft}^j-w_{f,t-1}^j$$
and the cumulative form makes connectivity constraints (between sectors, within airports, and in time) simple inequalities. The resulting zero-one program (p. 549-550) has capacity constraints per sector and period. Its LP relaxation is solved to optimality with all-integer solutions in the reported cases (p. 550-551), one of the observations that a strong formulation makes the relaxation nearly tight ([[Integer programming]]). A European instance with 2293 flights and 25 sectors was solved in about an hour, with total delay 60 percent below the first-come-first-served rule (p. 551).

## Job shop scheduling

Shows how structure plus cutting planes give strong bounds (BT §12.5, p. 551-562).

**Single machine** (p. 552-554). Jobs $i\in\mathcal N$ with processing times $p_i$ and weights $w_i$; minimize the weighted completion time $\sum w_iC_i$. Feasible completion times satisfy
$$C_j\ge p_j,\qquad C_j\ge C_k+p_j\ \ \text{or}\ \ C_k\ge C_j+p_k$$
The disjunction makes the feasible set nonconvex (Figure 12.2, a union of two disjoint polyhedra). Theorem 12.1 (p. 552): if $w_1/p_1\ge w_2/p_2\ge\dots\ge w_n/p_n$ then the order $1,\dots,n$ is optimal (an interchange argument). Applying it to the unit weights $w_i=p_i$ gives, for all $S\subset\mathcal N$ (eq. 12.1, p. 554, image seen):
$$\sum_{i\in S}p_iC_i\ge\frac12\sum_{i\in S}p_i^2+\frac12\Big(\sum_{i\in S}p_i\Big)^2$$
The book states, without proof, that these inequalities describe the convex hull of the feasible completion time vectors. The right side is $p(S)$, a supermodular function. For two jobs the hull is $C_1\ge p_1$, $C_2\ge p_2$ and $p_1C_1+p_2C_2\ge p_1^2+p_2^2+p_1p_2$. The single machine problem is then the LP of eq. (12.2), with exponentially many constraints.

**Separation** (Theorem 12.2, p. 555, read from text). Given $\mathbf C$, sort the jobs by increasing $C_i$ and let $S_i$ be the first $i$ jobs. Among $S_1,\dots,S_n$ pick $S_k$ maximizing
$$f(S)=p(S)-\sum_{i\in S}p_iC_i$$
If $f(S_k)\le0$, no inequality is violated. Otherwise $S_k$ gives a violated one. The proof shows a maximizer contains $j$ iff $C_j$ is below a threshold, so a maximizer is among the nested sets. Together with a cutting plane loop ([[Cutting-plane methods]]) the LP is solved in polynomial time, as promised by the separation-oracle theorem of the [[Ellipsoid method]].

Extensions (Example 12.3, p. 557): release times, deadlines and precedences enter as simple bounds on $C_i$, which makes the problem NP-hard, and the LP still gives a lower bound. A feasible schedule is built by sorting the LP optimum $\mathbf C^*$ and inserting idle time. Without release times and deadlines, the heuristic value satisfies (p. 558)
$$Z_{LP}\le Z_H\le2Z_{LP}$$
i.e. it is a 1-approximation algorithm, the best known bound for this NP-hard problem (see [[Integer programming]], approximation algorithms).

**Job shop** (p. 558-562). $n$ jobs, $m$ machines, task $(i,j)$ is stage $j$ of job $i$ with time $p_{ij}$. Minimize $\sum w_iC_{ik}$ subject to precedence of stages $C_{ij}\ge C_{i,j-1}+p_{ij}$ and, per machine $r$ with task set $M_r$, the single-machine inequalities of (12.1) for the tasks on that machine, solved by the same separation algorithm. The LP solution only gives an order on each machine, from which a heuristic builds a feasible schedule (p. 560), and branch and bound resolves conflicts by adding disjunctive constraints (Example 12.4: LP bound 23.67, final schedule cost 26, p. 561-562).

## Summary

Modeling, exploiting structure and fine tuning algorithms turn LP technology into usable decision support (BT §12.6, p. 562). Exercises 12.1-12.9 (p. 563-566) include stable matching, large job shop and TSP instances and a basketball line-up problem, all to be solved with a modeling language.

## Key facts
- The single machine convex hull is an example of the "exponentially many facets, polynomial separation" pattern that makes cutting plane methods work, shared with the spanning tree formulation of [[Integer programming]].
- Source credits (BT p. 567-568): Table 12.1 to Weber (1995); fleet assignment study by Hane et al.; air traffic model by Bertsimas and Stock (1997); single machine formulation by Queyranne (1993), the ordering rule of Theorem 12.1 by Smith (1956), the job shop heuristic motivated by Schulz (1996).
