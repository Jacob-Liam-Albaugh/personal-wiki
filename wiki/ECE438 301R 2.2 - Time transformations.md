# ECE438 301R 2.2 - Time transformations

**Summary**: ECE 301 review note on shifting, reversing and scaling the time axis of a signal, and on combining them in $x(at+b)$. The note stresses these are used throughout the course (source: Topic 2.2 time transformations.pdf, p. 1).

**Sources**: `Digital Signal Processing/301 Review/Topic 2.2 time transformations.pdf` (reading: OWN section 1.2.1).
**Last updated**: 2026-11-06

---

## The three basic transformations

1. Time shift $x(t-t_0)$, $t_0$ constant. For $t_0>0$ the signal is delayed and the plot moves right. For $t_0<0$ it is advanced by $|t_0|$ and moves left.
2. Time reversal $x(-t)$. A mirror image about the $t=0$ axis, like playing the signal backwards.
3. Time scaling $x(at)$, $a>0$. For $0<a<1$ the signal is stretched (slower). For $a>1$ it is squashed (faster).

(source: Topic 2.2, p. 1-3)

A signal with breakpoints $t_1,t_2$ then has: shift, breakpoints at $t_1+t_0,\,t_2+t_0$; reversal, at $-t_2,-t_1$; $x(3t)$, at $t_1/3,\,t_2/3$. Heights are unchanged (source: Topic 2.2, p. 5).

## Combining them

Three kinds of combination: reverse and scale (no problem), shift and scale such as $x(2t+2)$, reverse and shift such as $x(1-t)$ (source: Topic 2.2, p. 6).

Four approaches (source: Topic 2.2, p. 6):
1. Pure algebra: substitute $at+b$ for $t$ everywhere in the formula.
2. Find key change points of $x(t)$ and where each lands in the new signal.
3. Apply in the right order: to get $x(at+b)$ from $x(t)$, first shift by $b$, then scale by $a$.
4. Use the wrong order but adjust the shift (not suggested).

For $a=-1$ the author prefers approach 4: flip first, then shift by $-b$ (source: Topic 2.2, p. 8).

## Worked examples

> [!example] $x(2t+2)$ with $x(t)$ spanning $3$ to $6$
> Shift first: $x(t+2)$ spans $1$ to $4$. Then scale: $x(2t+2)$ spans $1/2$ to $2$. Approach 2 gives the same: solve $2c+2=3$, $2d+2=6$, so $c=1/2$, $d=2$ (source: Topic 2.2, p. 7).

> [!example] $x(1-t)$ with the same $x(t)$
> Shift then reverse gives support $-5$ to $-2$. Approach 2: $1-c=3$, $1-d=6$, so $c=-2$, $d=-5$. Approach 4: $x(-t)$ spans $-6$ to $-3$, shifting by $1$ gives $-5$ to $-2$ (source: Topic 2.2, pp. 7-8).

> [!example] $x(at+b)$ for $x(t)=t$ on $[0,1]$
> Substituting gives $at+b$ on $-b/a\le t\le(1-b)/a$ for $a>0$. The max value does not change (source: Topic 2.2, p. 8).

> [!example] $x(2t-5)$ and $x(5-2t)$
> $x(2t-5)$ is delayed by $5/2$ and squashed by 2: the point $2t-5=t_1$ is at $t=(t_1+5)/2$. For $x(5-2t)$ the breakpoints are at $t=-(t_1-5)/2$, a reversal plus scale plus shift (source: Topic 2.2, pp. 8-9).

> [!example] $x(t_0-t)$ with $x(t)=t\,[u(t)-u(t-1)]$
> Substitution gives $t_0-t$ for $t_0-1<t\le t_0$ and 0 otherwise. Graphically: flip first, then shift by $t_0$. Check: the height at $t=t_0-1$ is $t_0-(t_0-1)=1$ (source: Topic 2.2, p. 9-10). This is the pattern used inside [[Convolution]].
