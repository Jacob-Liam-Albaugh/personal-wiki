# ME697 2.5 - Active learning

**Summary**: [[Active learning]] picks the next training point where it is most valuable, so a surrogate needs fewer expensive evaluations. The book derives uncertainty sampling from MacKay's expected information gain, sketches value-of-information and multi-fidelity variants, and runs uncertainty sampling with a [[Gaussian process]] in 1D and 2D.

**File**: `advanced-scientific-machine-learning/book/up/al/`

---

## Topics
- **Intro**: takeaway only. Active learning reduces the labeled data a model needs by choosing the most informative samples.
- **01 Basics**: Bayesian setting; the general loop; information-theoretic acquisition (MacKay 1992); value of information; multi-fidelity acquisition; multi-objective optimization.
- **02 Hands-on**: uncertainty sampling with a fixed-hyperparameter GP in 1D and 2D (`tinygp`, `scipy.stats.qmc`).

## Key results
> [!definition] Setting (01)
> Regression $f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$ with prior $p(f)$ and likelihood $p(y\mid\mathbf x,f)$. Data $\mathcal D=\{(\mathbf x_i,y_i)\}$, conditionally independent given $f$. Posterior predictive:
> $$p(y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)=\int p(y\mid\mathbf x,f)\,p(f\mid\mathcal D)\,Df$$
> with mean $\mu(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal D)$ and variance $\sigma^2(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal D)$. Closed form for generalized linear models and GPs; otherwise Laplace approximation or posterior samples.

> [!algorithm] Active learning loop (01)
> 1. Start with a small, space-filling $\mathcal D_{n_0}$ and condition the model.
> 2. Find $\mathbf x_{t+1}=\arg\max_{\mathbf x}\alpha_t(\mathbf x)$, the information acquisition function.
> 3. If $\alpha_t(\mathbf x_{t+1})$ is below a threshold, stop.
> 4. Query $y_{t+1}=f_{\text{true}}(\mathbf x_{t+1})$, append to the data, re-condition.

> [!theorem] Expected information gain (01, after MacKay 1992)
> Information gain of a hypothetical observation $(\mathbf x,y)$ is the KL divergence from old to new posterior. Averaged over unknown $y$, it equals the drop in posterior entropy:
> $$\alpha_t(\mathbf x)=S_t-\mathbb E\big[S_{t+1}(\mathbf x,y)\mid\mathbf x\big]$$
> The reference measure $m(f)$ in the entropy cancels. With a Gaussian approximation of the posterior the book gets
> $$\alpha_t(\mathbf x)\approx\frac12\,\frac{\sigma_t^2(\mathbf x)}{\sigma^2}$$
> This is *uncertainty sampling*: query where the model is most uncertain. With input-dependent noise, divide by $\sigma^2(\mathbf x)$, so equal epistemic uncertainty favors the less noisy region.
> (source: `01_al_basics.md`, section "Information theoretic acquisition functions")

- **Boundary bias**: uncertainty sampling piles points at the edges of the domain, where the posterior is least constrained. MacKay gives other objectives targeting a region of interest.
- **Value of information**: a utility $u_t(\mathbf x,y)=v_t(y)-c_t(\mathbf x)$ (value of output minus cost). Acquisition is $\alpha_t(\mathbf x)=\mathbb E[u_t(\mathbf x,y)\mid\mathbf x]$. Expected improvement is a special case.
- **Multi-fidelity**: choose a fidelity $s$ and an input, $(s_{t+1},\mathbf x_{t+1})=\arg\max\alpha_t(s,\mathbf x)$. Example from the book:
$$\alpha_t(s,\mathbf x)=\lambda\frac{\sigma_{t,s}^2(\mathbf x)}{\sigma_s^2}-(1-\lambda)\,c_s(\mathbf x)$$
  $\lambda$ trades information against cost $c_s$. See [[ME697 2.4 - Multi-fidelity modeling]].
- **Multi-objective optimization**: hypervolume improvement as acquisition for finding a Pareto front (Beume et al. 2007; Pandita et al. 2018).

## Examples
> [!example] 1D uncertainty sampling (02)
> Data from $\sin(10x^2)+x$ plus noise ($0.1$). Fixed GP hyperparameters (RBF, amplitude 4, length scale 0.15, noise sd 0.1) because one point cannot train a model. The next point is the maximizer of the predictive variance, found by random search over 1000 [[Latin hypercube sampling]] points. First pick is near the boundary. Nine further points are added, one per step.

> [!example] 2D uncertainty sampling (02)
> A smooth blend of two functions (a sigmoid in $x_2-x_1$ mixes a sine/cosine term with a polynomial bump). Start from 5 LHS points, then 21 more steps with an anisotropic-capable RBF GP (length scales 0.3, noise sd 0.01). The variance map is highest at the domain edge, so new points land there first.

## Wiki notes (possible errors in the book)
- **Information gain formula (01)**: the expected gain under a Gaussian posterior is $\tfrac12\log\!\big(1+\sigma_t^2(\mathbf x)/\sigma^2\big)$ (MacKay 1992, from memory, **[unverified]**), not $\tfrac12\sigma_t^2/\sigma^2$. Both are increasing in $\sigma_t^2$, so the argmax, and hence uncertainty sampling, is unchanged. The book's form is only the small-variance limit.
- **Typo (01)**: `mu(\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{D}_{n_0})` is missing a backslash; it should be $\mu$.
- **Not a true argmax (02)**: `get_next_x` takes the best of 1000 random LHS points, so the pick is approximate, and it reuses LHS draws only to cover the box.
- **Hyperparameters stay fixed (02)**: the "optimally efficient" claim at the end holds for these hand-set values; refitting them as data arrives would change the variances.
- **Mixed units (01)**: the multi-fidelity example subtracts a cost from a dimensionless variance ratio, so $\lambda$ also sets the exchange rate between them.
